How long grain spawn takes to colonise

The window, the species that run fast and slow, the shake, and how to read a jar or bag that has stopped.

Grain spawn usually colonises in about 14 to 21 days at a warm room temperature. Oyster tends to finish at the fast end and shiitake runs slower. Shaking the jar or bag at roughly 20 to 30 percent colonised cuts days off. A jar with no visible growth after a week deserves a hard look.

Written by Sascha Heyer, who builds Mycro. Published 28 November 2026.

The window, and why it is only a window

For the gourmet and medicinal species most small farms grow, a jar or bag of grain spawn is usually fully colonised in about two to three weeks. That is an orientation, not a deadline. The same strain on the same grain can take ten days in a warm summer room and four weeks in a cold garage, and a bag inoculated from a liquid culture can beat one started from a single agar wedge by most of a week.

What the species changes is mainly the order: some run reliably faster than others.

SpeciesPace on grainWhat to expect
Oyster (blue, pearl, pink, king hybrids)FastUsually the quickest grain you will run: fast strains, black pearl included, can finish in 10 to 14 days
Lion's maneMediumFine, wispy growth that looks thin. Judge it by whether the kernels are bound together, not by how white it is
King oysterMediumSteady, dense growth
Chestnut, pioppinoMediumSteady; the bag may brown a little in places as it matures
ShiitakeSlowOften past three weeks. Brown patches on grain that has been running a while can be normal for shiitake

What changes the number

  • The inoculum. Grain grows outward from every point that was inoculated. A liquid culture spread through the grain, or a grain-to-grain transfer from a finished bag, starts from many points at once. A single agar wedge starts from one, so it takes longer.
  • Temperature. Most gourmet species run fastest in a room in the low to mid 20s °C (low to mid 70s °F). Colder rooms slow everything; much warmer ones favour bacteria over your culture. A full bag also warms itself as it grows, so do not pack bags tight in a warm cupboard.
  • The grain. Small kernels such as millet give more separate starting points per jar once it is shaken. Larger grains such as oats, rye or wheat hold more food per kernel and are cheaper. Both work.
  • Moisture. Grain that went in too wet is the classic cause of bacteria and sour jars. Grain that is too dry runs slowly and patchily.
  • Container size. A larger bag simply has further to go. Compare bags of the same size.
  • Gas exchange. A jar or bag needs a filter (a patch, a filter disc or a tight synthetic stuffing) so the culture can breathe without letting spores in.

The shake: when, and whether

Shake a jar or bag once the mycelium has covered roughly 20 to 30 percent of the grain. Breaking the colonised grain apart and spreading it through the rest gives the mycelium many new starting points instead of one advancing front, which is where the days are saved. Shake too early and a thin culture may not recover; shake too late and you gain little, because the grain is nearly run anyway.

The shake is optional. Plenty of growers never shake and accept a slower, undisturbed run, and some species (lion's mane above all) recover from it more slowly than oyster. If you do shake, write down the day: it is the only way to know later whether it actually helped.

When is grain spawn ready?

When it is white all the way through, the kernels are bound together into one mass, and you can no longer find untouched grain at the bottom or in the corners. Many growers give it a few more days after that so the mycelium consolidates. Do not leave it for weeks: overrun spawn starts using up its own food and can begin to pin inside the jar.

A rule of thumb that saves a lot of space on the shelf: a jar that shows colour does not get opened indoors, and a jar that has shown nothing for a week will rarely make up the time. The only number worth trusting is your own

Every figure on this page is a range from typical practice. Your real colonisation time depends on your room, your grain, your hydration and your culture, and the only way to know it is to write down, for every jar or bag, the day you made it, the day you shook it and the day it finished.

Frequently asked questions

How long does grain spawn take to colonize?

Usually about 14 to 21 days for a jar or bag of grain spawn kept at a warm room temperature. Oyster species tend to finish at the fast end of that window and shiitake runs noticeably slower, so a shiitake bag still going at three weeks is not a failure. The inoculum matters as much as the species: a liquid culture or a grain-to-grain transfer starts from many points at once and finishes sooner than a single agar wedge.

When should you shake grain spawn?

Once the mycelium has covered roughly 20 to 30 percent of the grain. By then it is strong enough to recover quickly, and breaking it up spreads colonised kernels through the rest of the jar or bag so it grows from many points instead of one front. Shake too early and a thin culture may stall; shake too late and you gain little, because the grain is nearly run. The shake is optional: plenty of growers skip it and accept a few extra days.

Is millet or oats faster for grain spawn?

Millet often finishes sooner after a shake, because its small kernels give the mycelium many more separate starting points in the same volume. Oats and other larger grains are cheaper per bag and easy to prepare, and hold more food per kernel. Which one is faster for you depends on your hydration, your sterilisation and your species, so compare them on your own bags rather than on a forum average.

Why is my grain spawn not colonizing?

If nothing has grown from the inoculation point after about a week, the most common causes are an inoculum that was dead or too small, grain that was too wet or too dry, or a room that is too cold. Growth that starts and then stops usually means bacteria or a competing mould is holding it back: look for cloudy, wet or sour-smelling grain at the bottom, or green and black patches. Those jars go out unopened.
