Are mushrooms on the FDA Food Traceability List?

What the food traceability rule (FSMA 204) means for a small gourmet mushroom farm, and the records worth keeping whether it applies or not.

No. Mushrooms are not on the FDA Food Traceability List, so a farm selling whole fresh mushrooms does not owe the extra FSMA 204 records for them. Sliced mushrooms are the open question: the list covers fresh-cut vegetables and FDA does not say either way. Enforcement starts no earlier than July 2028.

Written by Sascha Heyer, who builds Mycro. Published 19 November 2026. Checked against FDA's Food Traceability List page (content current as of 24 July 2026). Not legal advice.

What FSMA 204 is, in one paragraph

Section 204 of the Food Safety Modernization Act is the FDA's food traceability rule. For the foods on a published list, the Food Traceability List, everyone who grows, packs, ships, receives or transforms them has to keep specific records (FDA calls them key data elements) at specific steps (critical tracking events), and hand them over within 24 hours if FDA asks. The point is to trace an outbreak to its source in days instead of weeks. It applies to the foods on the list and to foods that contain a listed food as an ingredient in the same form, for example fresh.

What is on the list, and what is not

The list has 23 entries. Grouped, they are:

  • Fresh produce: cucumbers, herbs, leafy greens (fresh and fresh-cut), melons, peppers, sprouts, tomatoes, tropical tree fruits.
  • Fresh-cut produce: fresh-cut fruits, and fresh-cut vegetables other than leafy greens.
  • Other foods: shell eggs, nut butters, three kinds of soft or unpasteurised cheese, finfish (four entries, including smoked), crustaceans, molluscan shellfish, and refrigerated ready-to-eat deli salads.

Mushrooms are not named anywhere on it. A farm that grows oyster, lion's mane or shiitake and sells them whole and fresh, to a grocer, a restaurant or across a market table, is not growing a listed food. You may read elsewhere, usually on a software vendor's page, that mushrooms fall under FSMA 204. FDA's own list does not say so. Read the list on FDA's site yourself before you buy anything on the strength of that claim.

The open question: sliced mushrooms

One entry could reach mushrooms: vegetables other than leafy greens (fresh-cut). FDA describes it as all types of fresh-cut vegetables other than leafy greens, and says only that fresh-cut does not include previously frozen food. It does not name mushrooms and does not define fresh-cut further. So:

  • Whole mushrooms, trimmed at the stem and packed: not a listed food on any reading.
  • Sliced or diced mushrooms you pack for sale: could be read as fresh-cut vegetables. FDA has not said. Ask your buyer what they require, or use FDA's exemptions tool for your exact case.
  • Mushrooms you sell to someone who slices them or puts them in a deli salad: the business that cuts or mixes them is the one doing the transforming, and their buyer will tell them what records they need from you.

When it starts, and who is exempt

The original compliance date was 20 January 2026. Congress then directed FDA not to enforce the rule before 20 July 2028, and FDA says it intends to follow that directive. Even where a farm grows a listed food, FDA's FAQ exempts a produce farm whose average annual produce sales over the previous three years are no more than $25,000 (adjusted for inflation from 2020). There is no separate exemption for selling direct, so a market stall is not exempt on that ground alone. For a mushroom farm none of this decides anything unless you are selling a listed food.

Why a small mushroom farm keeps traceability anyway

Not being covered by a rule is not the same as not needing the records. Three ordinary days make them worth having:

  • A buyer asks which lot a box came from. Grocers and distributors increasingly ask for a lot or batch number on the case whether the law requires it or not. Enoki mushrooms have been recalled for Listeria more than once in recent years, and buyers remember.
  • Something goes wrong with a harvest. If a customer calls about a box, you need to know which batch it was, what day it was picked, and who else received that batch.
  • A culture goes bad. This is the one that costs a small farm money most often. A liquid culture or grain bag that turns out contaminated has usually already inoculated several bags. Knowing exactly which ones, instead of guessing, is the difference between binning six blocks and binning a shelf.

What to record: one step back, one step forward

The working principle of every traceability scheme, certified or not, is that each business knows where a thing came from one step back and where it went one step forward. On a mushroom farm that is five records:

  • Where the genetics came from. Supplier and their batch number, or where and when a wild sample was taken.
  • What came off what. A code on every plate, jar and grain bag that carries its parent, so SHI-01-P1-L1-G2 tells you which liquid culture and which plate that grain bag descends from.
  • A batch code on every block run, linked to the spawn it was made from.
  • The day each flush was picked and what it weighed.
  • Who received what: buyer, date, weight and batch code, ideally printed on the case label.

Frequently asked questions

Are mushrooms on the FDA Food Traceability List?

No. The FDA's Food Traceability List names leafy greens, herbs, cucumbers, melons, peppers, sprouts, tomatoes, tropical tree fruits, fresh-cut fruits and vegetables, shell eggs, nut butters, some soft cheeses, finfish, crustaceans, molluscan shellfish and ready-to-eat deli salads. Mushrooms are not named on it, so a farm selling whole fresh mushrooms does not owe the extra FSMA 204 records for them. The list can change, so check FDA's own page before relying on this.

Does FSMA 204 apply to sliced mushrooms?

Possibly, and FDA has not said. The list includes fresh-cut vegetables other than leafy greens, and FDA describes that entry only as all types of fresh-cut vegetables, without naming mushrooms or defining fresh-cut beyond excluding previously frozen food. If you slice, dice or pack mushrooms for sale, or supply someone who does, ask your buyer or use FDA's exemptions tool rather than assume either way.

When does the food traceability rule take effect?

The original compliance date was 20 January 2026. Congress then directed FDA not to enforce the rule before 20 July 2028, and FDA has said it intends to follow that directive, so in practice enforcement starts no earlier than July 2028 for the foods on the list.

Do small mushroom farms need traceability records at all?

Not for FSMA 204, but most keep a simple version anyway, because a grocery buyer can ask which lot a box came from, a recall needs you to know who received it, and a contaminated culture is only useful to find if you know what came off it. A batch code on every block, the day each flush was picked, and who received how much of which batch covers almost every question a buyer or an inspector would ask.
